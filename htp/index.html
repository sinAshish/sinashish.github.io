Sparse slices leave gaps

In the paper's main setting, one slice in four is annotated. On the annotated slices the contour is known. Between them, many different 3D surfaces fit the same labels.

A neural signed distance function picks one of them by whatever bias it has. In practice that means bumps, necks, and thin bridges between vessels that run close together.

A prior has to say what the space between the slices should look like. For vessels the answer is short: a tube.

Penalizing curvature punishes thin vessels

The usual way to ask for a smooth surface is to penalize mean curvature, H. On a tube of radius r, H is 1/(2r). Halve the radius and H doubles, so the squared penalty grows four times.

A small vessel is the cheapest thing to delete. The network learns to shrink thin vessels away, and in the clinic those are often the ones that matter.

A distance function hides a zero in its Hessian

The surface is stored as a signed distance function f. It is positive outside, negative inside, and zero on the surface. Its gradient is the surface normal n.

The Hessian of f is the matrix of second derivatives. It holds the bending. The obvious score is its determinant, which is the textbook Gaussian curvature.

That score is always zero. A distance function grows at slope one along its own normal, so its second derivative in that direction is zero. One eigenvalue is zero, so the determinant is zero. On a tube, on a ball, on a sheet.

Project out the normal

The fix is to ignore the normal direction. Project the Hessian onto the tangent plane with P, then divide by the gradient length. The result is the shape operator S. It has two eigenvalues, the principal curvatures.

Shape operatorS=P∇2fP‖∇f‖,P=I−nnT

On a tube, the surface follows a circle around it, so κ₁ is 1/r. Along the tube it is straight, so κ₂ is 0. Their product K is zero at any radius, which is what we wanted.

But K is also zero on a flat sheet. And a sheet is what you get when two nearby vessels fuse.

The left arc is the bend around the vessel (κ₁ = 1/r). The right arc is the bend along it (κ₂). Drag t in the next section to change it.

Divide by the mean curvature

So the score divides K by the square of H. A tube scores zero. A blob, which bends equally both ways, scores one. A neck, which bends in opposite directions, scores below zero.

Because K and H² both scale as 1/r², the radius cancels. Slide t, then change the radius. The score does not move.

Sheets and floaters need their own terms

Because a sheet scores zero too, the loss adds a second term. It charges any surface that is flatter than the widest vessel in the data. Tubes and necks are untouched.

A separate term acts away from the surface. It removes floaters, stray blobs of surface in empty space, and it is the only place the prior touches the background.

The Eikonal term keeps f a distance function. It does not by itself make the surface smooth or connected.

Switch it off where the labels already speak

On an annotated slice the answer is known. A prior there can only fight the labels. So the loss is gated by distance from the nearest annotated slice.

Gateg(x)=1−exp(−(dobsσg)2)
Gated lossℒtube=𝔼x∈shell[g(x)etube(x)]
dobs
distance from the point to the nearest annotated slice, not to a contour
σg
how fast the gate opens. One σg out, g = 0.63. Two out, g = 0.98.

The paper uses σg of one voxel. A small σg lets the prior reach the labels. A large σg keeps it from turning fully on even mid-gap.

Removing the gate costs accuracy even with full labels: Dice drops from 0.977 to 0.923 at k = 1.

Where it stops

The prior is local. It scores each point from its own curvature, so it cannot enforce connectivity across wide gaps such as k = 16. A learned global prior may help there. Local attributes such as tortuosity may need extra guidance.
